GA4 Demo Ecommerce · BigQuery Public Dataset · Nov 2020 – Jan 2021

Where the conversion problem actually lives

Three independent cuts of the same 92-day window — by acquisition channel, by product, and by new-vs-returning user — each isolating a different piece of the same underlying issue.

All figures below come from the bigquery-public-data.ga4_obfuscated_sample_ecommerce public dataset. This is a separate, unrelated dataset from the GA4 Demo Account UI exploration used elsewhere in this project — the two are not reconciled. No ad-spend or cost data exists in this dataset, so nothing here should be read as ROI, ROAS, or profit.
Story One

Acquisition Channel Performance

If one acquisition channel converted far worse than the rest, the fix would be to change the media mix. That's not what the data shows.

What this rules out
Six of the seven channels cluster tightly between 6.63% and 7.19% — no meaningful difference between paid search, organic search, direct visits, or referral. The one outlier is shop.googlemerchandisestore at 8.96%, noticeably higher than the rest. That's explainable rather than mysterious: this "channel" is effectively the store's own domain, so anyone arriving this way has already navigated straight into the shop — a small, high-intent, self-selected slice of traffic rather than a real acquisition channel in the usual sense. Outside that one exception, the acquisition mix itself is not the problem: whatever is suppressing conversion has to be happening after the click, not before it.
Story Two

Product Portfolio Analysis

Every product grouped by what it actually is — apparel, drinkware, stationery & office, or everything else — to see whether traffic and conversion behave differently by product type, not just by an abstract traffic/conversion split.

View traffic vs. conversion rate, all 413 products, by category

x-axis is logarithmic (traffic spans 30–14,713 views) · same-item conversion: viewers of a product who bought that exact product · categories assigned by product name, reviewed by hand
Reading story one and two together

Site-wide, 6.6%–9% of viewers from every channel go on to buy something. But on a same-item basis, the 157 highest-traffic products (mostly apparel) convert at just 0.7% on average. Users aren't leaving empty-handed; they're leaving the product page they arrived on. That points at the product page and cross-sell experience, not the acquisition channel, as where the leak is.

The category view adds a second, independent signal: Apparel is both the single largest category (159 products, 49% of all product-page traffic) and the lowest-converting one (1.11% average) — while Drinkware, a much smaller slice of traffic, converts 65% better (1.83%). Channel doesn't explain that gap (Story One), and it isn't just "popular items convert worse" either, since Drinkware includes some high-traffic SKUs too. What the category and the item both point to (Story Two, seen two ways) is that apparel pages specifically — not traffic volume in general — are where conversion underperforms.

The step that doesn't fit common sense
Most of this funnel behaves exactly as expected: people browse without buying, then thin out further while comparison-shopping before checkout. The step worth scrutinizing is the last one — begin_checkout → purchase. Once someone has already committed to checking out, common sense says most should finish, but a large share of both cohorts don't. This dataset can't say why on its own, but it raises real questions worth testing: is the checkout form asking for too much? Does tax/shipping added at the last step create sticker shock? Is there a technical failure point in the flow? This is a hypothesis for follow-up research (session recordings, checkout-abandonment surveys), not a conclusion the data proves by itself.
    Cross-Cutting

    Does Category Change the Channel or the Journey?

    Story One found every channel converts about the same, and Story Three found a surprising checkout drop-off. This section asks whether either of those holds true within every product category, or only on average.

    Channel mix by product category

    Share of each category's product-page viewers, by first-touch channel · "(data deleted)" rows excluded
    The channel mix doesn't change
    Every category draws traffic from the same channels in almost exactly the same proportions — google/organic is ~34–35% of viewers for Apparel, Drinkware, Stationery & Office, and Other alike, and every other channel lines up the same way within a percentage point. Users don't reach different kinds of products through different channels. Whatever varies across categories, it isn't acquisition.

    Step completion rate by category

    Share of users at one step who reach the next
    The journey does change — specifically at checkout
    Apparel shoppers who add an item to cart are actually the most likely of any category to proceed to checkout (40%, vs. 25–32% for the others) — there's no hesitation getting into the checkout flow. But once there, Apparel has by far the worst completion rate: only 66% of Apparel checkouts finish as a purchase, compared with 74% for Stationery & Office, 79% for Other, and 85% for Drinkware. That's the most specific finding in this whole analysis: it isn't "checkout is a little broken everywhere" (Story Three) so much as "checkout is disproportionately broken for apparel" — which, combined with Apparel being the largest and lowest-converting category (Story Two), makes apparel checkout the single most concrete place to start investigating.

    Putting the three stories together

    1. Acquisition is not the problem. Every real channel converts within a tight 6.6%–9% band, so redirecting budget between channels would not move the needle.
    2. The leak is concentrated in specific, identifiable products. 157 of 413 products absorb 76% of all product-page traffic but convert at under 1% on a same-item basis, while a large "hidden gem" group of lower-traffic products converts 7x better — these 157 SKUs are a short, actionable list for page-level optimization.
    3. Checkout itself has a disproportionate drop, for new and returning users alike. The biggest unexplained loss isn't at the top of the funnel — it's after a user has already decided to check out, which points at friction in the checkout flow rather than in product interest.
    4. That checkout problem is concentrated in Apparel specifically. Apparel shoppers reach checkout at the highest rate of any category, then complete it at the lowest (66%, vs. 74–85% elsewhere) — and channel mix is identical across categories, so this isn't an acquisition issue either. Apparel's checkout flow is the single most targeted place to investigate first.
